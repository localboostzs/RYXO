Caffeine and sleep: what the research says

Published: October 26, 2026

Caffeine is one of the most widely used stimulants in the world, and a big reason why people reach for coffee, tea and energy drinks. It is also one of the most common reasons for a bad night. Here is what the research and the European authorities say, in plain language.

How caffeine works

Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors in the brain. Adenosine is a substance that builds up during the day and makes you feel sleepy. When caffeine blocks it, you feel more awake and alert — until the caffeine is cleared from your body.

How long does it stay in your body?

In healthy adults, the half-life of caffeine — the time the body needs to clear half of it — is commonly cited as about 5 hours, with a range of roughly 3 to 7 hours between people. It can be longer during pregnancy, with some medicines such as certain hormonal contraceptives, and in people with liver conditions. That means a drink in the late afternoon can still be partly active at bedtime.

What do EFSA’s numbers say?

The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) assessed the safety of caffeine in 2015 and published these reference points:

  • Up to 400 mg per day from all sources: no safety concern for healthy adults (pregnant women excluded).
  • Single doses up to 200 mg: no safety concern for healthy adults.
  • Pregnant and breast-feeding women: up to 200 mg per day from all sources.
  • Children and adolescents: up to 3 mg per kg of body weight per day.
  • Sleep: even 100 mg of caffeine may affect sleep duration and sleep patterns in some adults, particularly when consumed close to bedtime.

These are safety reference values, not recommendations to consume that much. Sensitivity differs a lot from person to person.

Where does caffeine hide?

Coffee, tea, cola, energy drinks, some sports and pre-workout products, chocolate and cocoa, and some medicines. The amount varies strongly between products and serving sizes, so check the label where there is one.

Practical tips

  • Many sleep experts suggest stopping caffeine several hours before bedtime, often six hours or more. Your own sensitivity may call for more or less.
  • Notice your own reaction: if you fall asleep slowly or wake often, try a few days without afternoon caffeine.
  • Reach for caffeine-free options in the evening: water, herbal tea, caffeine-free soft drinks or a caffeine-free energy drink.
  • If sleep problems persist, talk to a doctor. This guide does not replace medical advice.
